How to read this document

This document deliberately asks more than it answers. In a federation of independent societies, most of the
difficult decisions are commercial and governance decisions rather than technical ones. Recording them
explicitly now is far cheaper than discovering the disagreement during build or, worse, after go-live.

1. Purpose and how to use this document

The proposed system serves a federation, not a chain. The central cooperative and each member cooperative
shop are separate legal entities. That single fact changes the nature of almost every transaction in the system:
goods moving from central to a shop are not an internal stock transfer but a commercial transaction between
two businesses, and data generated at a shop is that shop's own commercial record rather than central's.

The questions below are grouped by domain. Within each domain, questions are numbered with a section
letter and sequence number so they can be referenced unambiguously in discussion and in the answers you
return.

Section A. Commercial and ownership model

   Why this section comes first

   This is the most consequential section in the document. Because central and each shop are separate legal
   entities, goods moving between them is a real commercial transaction. Whether ownership passes on delivery
   or is retained until sale determines the inventory model, the accounting entries, the credit risk, and who
   absorbs losses. Nearly every later section inherits from A-01.

A-01 When central supplies goods to a shop, does ownership pass to the shop at that moment (firm sale), or
          does central retain ownership until the shop sells the item to an end customer (consignment)?
           Why we ask: This determines whose balance sheet the stock sits on, when central recognises revenue, whether
           central can recall unsold stock, and who carries the loss when goods expire or are damaged on the shelf. The
           inventory model, the ledger postings and the credit exposure all follow from this one answer.

A-02 Could the model differ by product category, for example firm sale for dry goods but consignment for
          high-value or short-shelf-life items?
           Why we ask: If the model is mixed, every stock lot must carry an ownership flag rather than the system applying
           one global rule. That is considerably more complex and is far better known now than discovered during build.

A-03 At what precise point does risk of loss transfer: when goods leave the central warehouse, when the
          vehicle arrives at the shop, or when the shop signs the GRN?
           Why we ask: Determines who absorbs in-transit damage and theft, and therefore which party the system charges
           when a delivery arrives short or damaged.

A-04 Is a shop obliged to accept and pay for stock that central allocates to it, or may it decline a delivery or
          return unsold stock?
           Why we ask: Governs whether a returns-to-central workflow and a credit note mechanism are needed, and
           whether central can push stock or only respond to pull.

A-05 If returns to central are permitted, under what conditions: time window, condition of goods, restocking
          charge, prior authorisation?

A-06 At what price do shops buy from central: a wholesale or transfer price, cost plus a fixed margin, or a
          member-discounted rate?

A-07 Is there a single price list for all shops, or do transfer prices vary by shop, region, or volume purchased?
           Why we ask: Volume-tiered or regional pricing requires a price-list engine with effective-dated versions rather
           than a single price field on the product record.

A-08 May shops purchase the same goods from outside suppliers if the price is better, or must they source
          exclusively from central where central stocks the item?
           Why we ask: Determines whether the system must record a parallel non-central supply chain per shop, and
           whether central can treat shop requisitions as a reliable proxy for true demand when planning procurement.

A-09 What are the payment terms: does the shop pay before dispatch, on delivery, or on credit? If on credit,
          over what period?

A-10 Is there a credit limit per shop, and should the system block a requisition that would breach it, or merely
          warn?
           Why we ask: A hard block has real operational consequences, since a shop that cannot order cannot trade. The
           rule and its escalation path must be agreed by the federation before it is built, not chosen by a developer.

A-11 Who may override a credit block, and must that approval be recorded for audit?

Section B. SKU definition and product master data

   On the SKU question

   You asked what the scope of an SKU should be. This is the right question, and it is a decision rather than a
   lookup. An SKU (Stock Keeping Unit) is the smallest thing the business agrees to count as distinct. Every
   stock balance, sales report, reorder point and reconciliation aggregates at this level, so if central and the
   shops draw the boundary differently, no report between them will ever agree.

B-01 At what level is a distinct SKU defined? Please confirm the exact attribute set that makes two items
          different SKUs: brand, product, pack size, variant, flavour, colour, grade?
           Why we ask: This is the foundation of the entire data model. It should be written down as a rule that a warehouse
           clerk and a shop assistant would both apply the same way.

B-02 Is a case, carton or outer pack a separate SKU from the single selling unit, or the same SKU with a
          defined pack conversion factor?
           Why we ask: The most common source of stock error in distribution systems. Central habitually thinks in cases
           while the shop counter sells singles. Whichever answer is chosen, the conversion must be held in one place and
           be unambiguous.

B-03 If the same product is bought from two different suppliers or manufacturers, is that one SKU or two?
           Why we ask: One SKU simplifies selling and shelf management but hides supplier-level cost and quality
           differences. Two SKUs preserve traceability but fragment stock balances and sales reporting.

B-04 Are batch number, manufacture date and expiry date attributes of the stock holding rather than of the
          SKU itself? Which product categories genuinely require batch or expiry tracking?
           Why we ask: Batch tracking multiplies the size of the inventory records and changes the picking rule from FIFO
           to FEFO. It should be applied only where it is needed, because it adds counting effort at every shop.

B-05 Are any items serialised, that is tracked by an individual unique number, for example appliances
          carrying a warranty?

B-06 Are loose or weighed goods sold, such as rice, dhal, sugar or vegetables broken down from bulk? If so,
          how is the bulk purchase unit converted to a retail selling unit, and how is natural weight loss or gain
          accounted for?
           Why we ask: Bulk-to-loose conversion creates a permanent and expected variance that is neither damage nor
           theft. Without a distinct transaction type for it, stock will never balance and shrinkage figures will be meaningless.

B-07 Who has authority to create a new SKU in the central catalogue, and what approval does it require?

B-08 May a shop create its own SKU for a locally sourced item that central does not supply?

B-09 If shops may create local SKUs, should these sit in a separate numbering range from central SKUs, and
          should central be able to see them?
           Why we ask: A separate range keeps the shared catalogue clean and prevents two shops inventing conflicting
           codes for the same product. Whether central can see them at all is a visibility decision that belongs with Section
           M.

B-10 If a locally sourced item is later adopted into the central range, is there a process to promote or merge
          the local SKU into the central catalogue without losing that shop's sales history?
           Why we ask: Merging product codes retrospectively is difficult if not designed for. Deciding now avoids a
           permanent break in trend data later.

B-11 Do products carry barcodes reliably? What is done for unbarcoded and loose items: printed shelf labels,
          in-store printed barcodes, or PLU codes keyed at the till?
           Why we ask: This determines the scanning workflow and the hardware specification at every shop, including

B-12 Can one SKU carry multiple barcodes, for example when a supplier changes pack artwork or an item is
          sourced from more than one market?

B-13 What set of units of measure (UOM) is required, and who defines conversion factors: central only, or
          may a shop define its own?

B-14 Must product names, descriptions and categories be held in all three languages in the master catalogue,
          and who is responsible for supplying and maintaining the translations?
           Why we ask: Trilingual master data is a continuing content operation rather than a one-time technical feature.
           For a catalogue of any size it needs a named owner and a budget, otherwise it degrades into partial coverage
           within months.

B-15 What product category hierarchy is required, and is it common across the federation, or may shops
          re-categorise items for their own merchandising?
           Why we ask: A shared hierarchy is what makes cross-shop comparison possible. Local re-categorisation makes
           shops happier but breaks federation-level analysis unless a mapping is maintained.

B-16 Are there items that are restricted or age-controlled, or that some shops are not permitted to sell?

B-17 How are seasonal or discontinued items handled, and can an SKU be deactivated centrally while a shop
          still holds stock of it?
           Why we ask: Deactivation while stock remains on shelves is a routine cause of tills refusing to sell saleable
           goods. The rule needs to be explicit.

Section C. Requisition, allocation and fulfilment

C-01 What triggers a requisition: manual entry by shop staff, a system suggestion based on reorder point and
          sales history, or both?

C-02 Does central approve every requisition, or is it fulfilled automatically when stock is available? Who
          approves, and are there value thresholds requiring higher approval?

C-03 When total requested quantity exceeds available central stock, how is the shortfall allocated across
          shops: first come first served, pro rata to request, share of historical sales, or a fixed quota?
           Why we ask: This is a fairness question between independent member societies, with real commercial
           consequences for the shops that lose out. It should be decided by the federation and recorded in policy, not
           settled by whichever rule is easiest to code.

C-04 Should the allocation rule be configurable by central at any time, or fixed and changeable only by formal
          decision?

C-05 Is partial fulfilment permitted, and does the unfulfilled remainder stay open as a backorder or get
          cancelled?

C-06 Is there a minimum order quantity or minimum order value per requisition, per line, or per delivery run?

C-07 Are requisitions raised on a fixed cycle, for example each shop ordering on an assigned weekday, or ad
          hoc at any time?
           Why we ask: A fixed cycle lets central batch picking and plan delivery routes efficiently. Ad hoc ordering
           demands continuous fulfilment capacity. This shapes the warehouse operation as much as the software.

C-08 May a shop amend or cancel a requisition after submission, and up to what point in the fulfilment
          process?

C-09 Are emergency or out-of-cycle requests permitted, and do they follow a different approval path or attract
          a different delivery charge?

C-10 May shops transfer stock directly to one another, or must all movement route through central?
           Why we ask: A lateral transfer between two independent cooperatives is commercially a sale between two
           entities, requiring its own invoice and settlement. It also requires each shop to see something of another shop's
           stock position, which is a visibility decision.

C-11 If lateral transfers are permitted, does central approve them, and at what price do they transact?

C-12 Should a shop be able to see central stock availability before raising a requisition, and should that be a
          live figure or an indicative one?
           Why we ask: A live figure is more useful but invites shops to race each other for scarce stock, which interacts
           with the allocation rule in C-03.

C-13 What lead time does central commit to between requisition and delivery, and should the system
          measure performance against it?

Section D. Delivery, goods receipt and discrepancies

D-01 Who performs delivery: a central fleet, a contracted third-party logistics provider, or does the shop
          collect from a depot?

D-02 Are deliveries planned as routes serving several shops from one vehicle?
           Why we ask: If so, the system needs load planning and a per-drop delivery note rather than a simple per-shop
           dispatch document.

D-03 Should drivers use a mobile application for proof of delivery, or is a signed paper delivery order
          sufficient?

D-04 At goods receipt, does the shop count every line, or accept on trust and count later?

D-05 When the received quantity differs from the dispatched quantity, what happens? Does the shop record
          what it actually received and raise a discrepancy for central to investigate, or must the receipt match
          the dispatch note?
           Why we ask: This decides whether a shop's stock ledger may temporarily diverge from central's records. Forcing
           an exact match makes reconciliation trivial but causes shops to carry stock on the system that they do not
           physically have, which corrupts every subsequent count.

D-06 Who investigates a disputed discrepancy, who adjudicates it, and within what time limit must it be
          raised?

D-07 When goods arrive damaged, is the shop credited automatically on receipt, or only after inspection and
          approval by central?

D-08 Should the receiving clerk be able to photograph damaged or short deliveries and attach the images to
          the discrepancy record?
           Why we ask: Photographic evidence captured at the moment of receipt substantially reduces disputes between
           independent entities, and is inexpensive if designed in from the start rather than added later.

D-09 Should vehicle, driver and delivery timestamp be recorded for accountability?

D-10 Are returnable containers, crates or pallets in circulation that must be tracked and reconciled?
           Why we ask: Unreturned containers are a genuine and often unmeasured cost. If they are in use, they need their
           own balance per shop.

Section E. Inventory management

E-01 How many central warehouses or regional depots exist, and does stock move between them?

E-02 Within a shop, must stock be distinguished by location, for example sales floor versus back store?
           Why we ask: This is what lets a shop answer the question 'we have none on the shelf, is there any in the back'. It
           also introduces replenishment tasks, so it should be adopted deliberately rather than by default.

E-03 What costing method should apply: weighted average, FIFO, or standard cost?
           Why we ask: This determines reported cost of goods sold and therefore each shop's declared profit. It has tax
           consequences and should be confirmed with the accountants who sign off the societies' books.

E-04 For batch-tracked goods, is the picking rule FIFO or FEFO?

E-05 How often are physical stock counts performed? Is cycle counting, that is counting a rotating subset
          continuously, acceptable, or are only full stocktakes recognised?

E-06 Must trading stop during a stocktake, or should the system support counting while the shop continues to
          trade?
           Why we ask: Counting while trading is significantly more complex but avoids closing shops, which for a small
           society may mean a day of lost revenue.

E-07 Who may approve a stock adjustment arising from a count, and above what variance value must it be
          escalated?

E-08 Should reorder points and safety stock be calculated by the system for each shop and SKU, or entered
          manually by the shop?

E-09 Should the system permit negative stock, that is selling an item the records say is not present, or block
          the sale?
           Why we ask: Blocking enforces data discipline but will stop genuine sales at the counter whenever records are
           wrong, which in early operation is often. Most retailers permit it and manage it through an exception report. This
           interacts directly with offline selling in Section H.

E-10 Are there items with special storage requirements, such as cold chain, that the system should track or
          alert on?

E-11 Should stock ageing be reported, showing how long stock has been held?
           Why we ask: Ageing is the earliest warning of over-ordering, and under a consignment model it is central's
           exposure rather than the shop's.

Section F. Damages, discards, expiry and shrinkage

   Coupled to the ownership decision

   You raised this and it deserves treatment as a full domain. It is tightly coupled to Section A: under
   consignment, central absorbs most of these losses; under firm sale, the shop does. Until A-01 is answered,
   F-02 cannot be.

F-01 Which loss categories must be recorded and reported separately? For example: damaged in transit,
          damaged in store, expired, spoiled, pest or weather damage, theft, unexplained shrinkage, staff
          consumption, samples and tasting, donation or charitable write-off, and returns to supplier.
           Why we ask: Each has a different owner, a different accounting treatment and a different management response.
           Recording them all as a single 'adjustment' makes the data worthless for deciding what to fix.

F-02 Under the ownership model chosen in A-01, who bears the cost of each loss category: central, the shop,
          an insurer, the transporter, or the supplier?

F-03 May damaged or expired goods be returned to central or to the supplier for credit, and is there a claim
          workflow to support that?

F-04 What approval is required to write off stock, and does the requirement vary by value?

F-05 Must written-off goods be physically verified and witnessed before disposal, and should the system
          record who witnessed it?
           Why we ask: Write-off is the most common vector for stock fraud in retail, because it makes missing goods
           legitimate on paper. Dual approval and a witness record are standard controls and are far cheaper to include from
           the outset.

F-06 Should stock approaching expiry trigger alerts, and at what lead time by product category?

F-07 Is markdown selling of near-expiry goods permitted, and should a markdown be recorded as a distinct
          event separate from an ordinary discount?
           Why we ask: Separating the two lets you measure the true cost of over-ordering, which is otherwise hidden
           inside general discounting and never surfaces to management.

F-08 Is a disposal or destruction certificate required for any category, such as food, pharmaceutical or
          agrochemical goods?

F-09 Should shrinkage be reported as a percentage measure per shop, and is that figure visible to central or
          only to the shop itself?
           Why we ask: This is a politically sensitive metric between independent societies. It belongs in the visibility matrix
           in Section M rather than being decided by default.

F-10 Are there acceptable tolerance levels for loss by category, below which no investigation is required?

F-11 Should the system distinguish goods that are damaged but still saleable at a reduced price from goods
          that are unsaleable?

Section G. Pricing

G-01 Does central set the retail selling price, recommend it, or leave it entirely to each shop?

G-02 If central sets or recommends prices, may a shop deviate? Does deviation require approval, or merely a
          recorded reason?

G-03 Are any goods subject to government-controlled maximum retail prices? If so, must the system enforce
          the ceiling and retain a history of controlled prices?
           Why we ask: Selling above a gazetted maximum price is a legal offence, so this is a hard control rather than a
           preference. It also requires effective-dated price records so that a past transaction can be shown to have been
           compliant at the time.

G-04 Are there member prices distinct from non-member prices? If so, are they funded by the shop or by
          central?

G-05 Are promotions required: time-limited price reductions, multi-buy offers, basket-level discounts,
          bundles?

G-06 Who funds a promotion, central or the shop, and must the system apportion the cost between them?
           Why we ask: Between independent entities, promotional funding is a real settlement item rather than a display
           detail. If central funds a promotion, the shop must be reimbursed, and that calculation has to come from
           somewhere.

G-07 May till operators apply manual discounts? Within what limits, and with what authorisation?

G-08 Should price changes be effective-dated and scheduled in advance, so that a shop can load next
          month's prices ahead of time?
           Why we ask: Without scheduling, price changes must be applied manually on the day, which across many shops

G-09 Are prices inclusive or exclusive of tax at the shelf edge, and is that consistent across all shops?

Section H. Point of sale and offline operation

   Offline is a requirement, not an option

   You have confirmed connectivity will be provided but that outages and slow links are expected in remote
   areas. That makes offline capability a requirement rather than an option. These questions establish how far it
   must extend, which is the main cost variable in this section.

H-01 What is the longest offline period the till must survive while continuing to trade: an hour, a full trading
          day, several days?
           Why we ask: An hour can be handled with a simple queue held in memory. Several days requires a full local
           database at each till, a locally held product and price catalogue, and a designed conflict-resolution strategy. The
           difference in cost and complexity is large, so the answer should reflect the worst real site rather than the average
           one.

H-02 While offline, which operations must still work: sale, refund, member lookup, price lookup, card payment,
          receipt printing, stock deduction?

H-03 Which operations may legitimately be blocked while offline?

H-04 If a shop and central both change the same record while disconnected, whose version prevails on
          reconnection?

Section J. Inter-entity billing and accounting

   On AR and AP

   Every supply from central creates a matched pair of entries: an account receivable (AR) at central, being
   money owed to it, and an account payable (AP) at the shop, being money it owes. Because these sit in the
   books of two different legal entities, they must agree, and disagreement between them is the routine source of
   disputes in federated structures. The scope question in J-01 decides how much of that ledger the system
   owns.

J-01 Should the system produce a full set of accounts for each shop, that is general ledger, trial balance,
          profit and loss and balance sheet? Or only manage the receivable and payable between central and
          each shop, exporting to whatever books each society already keeps?
           Why we ask: One of the largest scope decisions in the programme. Full multi-entity accounting roughly doubles
           the finance scope and brings each society's statutory reporting inside the system. Many federations prefer to
           leave society bookkeeping where it is and integrate instead.

J-02 If only AR and AP are in scope, what export format is required, and to which accounting packages do the
          shops currently post?

J-03 What is the applicable tax regime: VAT, SVAT, or another? Which items are exempt or zero-rated, and
          does the treatment differ between central and shops?

J-04 Is government electronic invoicing or fiscal reporting mandated now, or expected in the near term?
           Why we ask: If it is coming, invoice numbering, immutability and archival must be designed for it from the start.
           Retrofitting fiscal compliance into a live retail system is disproportionately expensive.

J-05 What document numbering rules apply, and must numbering be sequential and gapless within each
          entity?
           Why we ask: Gapless sequential numbering is a common statutory requirement and it conflicts with offline
           operation, since a disconnected till cannot know the next number. If it is required, the design must reserve
           number ranges per site in advance.

J-06 What is the retention period for transaction and tax records?

J-07 Are credit notes and debit notes required, and who may issue them?

J-08 Does the financial year end differ between central and any of the shops?

J-09 Who audits each society, and are there audit requirements the system must support, such as read-only
          auditor access or a defined transaction listing?

Section K. Membership and patronage

   Why members matter

   You asked how members matter to the system. In a cooperative, members are owners rather than ordinary
   customers, and the relationship often carries financial consequences: preferential prices, patronage rebates
   on the year's purchases, share capital, credit facilities, and voting rights. If any of these exist, purchases must
   be attributed to a member identity at the till, which changes the checkout flow and the year-end close. If none
   do, this section falls away and the system is simply retail. Both are legitimate answers, but it must be
   answered explicitly.

K-01 Does the business need to distinguish members from ordinary customers at the point of sale at all?
           Why we ask: If the answer is no, questions K-02 to K-08 do not apply and a significant amount of scope
           disappears.

K-02 Does each shop maintain its own member register, or is there an islandwide member identity shared
          across the federation?
           Why we ask: If a member of one society may shop at another, the system needs a federated identity and a rule
           for which society the patronage accrues to. If not, the register stays local and much simpler.

K-03 Do members receive preferential prices, and are those funded by the shop or by central?

K-04 Are patronage rebates or dividends paid based on a member's purchases over the year, and must this
          system calculate them?
           Why we ask: This requires every relevant sale to be reliably attributed to a member, which puts an identification
           step into the checkout flow and slows the queue. The operational cost is real and should be weighed against the
           benefit.

K-05 Is member share capital, subscription or savings tracked, and is that in scope here or held in a separate
          system?

K-06 Are there member loans or credit facilities to administer?

K-07 Is any governance function expected in the system, such as membership records for AGM voting
          eligibility, or is that entirely outside scope?

K-08 What personal data is held about members, and what consent and privacy obligations apply to it?
           Why we ask: Member records are personally identifiable information belonging to an independent society. Who
           may see them across the federation is a Section M question with legal weight.

Section M. Data ownership, visibility and governance

   What this section is really asking

   To clarify the earlier question: this is not about whether to use one central database or a separate database
   per shop. That is an architecture choice made afterwards, and strict ownership rules can be honoured
   perfectly well inside a single shared database. The question here is the legal and governance one. Because
   each society is an independent entity, its transaction data is its own commercial record, and the federation
   must decide who owns it, who may see it, and what happens to it if a society leaves. The technology can
   implement whatever is decided, but it cannot make the decision.

M-01 Who legally owns the transaction data generated at a shop: the shop as an independent entity, central,
          or both jointly?
           Why we ask: This governs what central may do with the data, what happens if a society leaves the federation,
           and who is answerable to a regulator or data protection authority. It is a constitutional question for the federation
           and should be settled in writing before the system encodes an answer by default.

M-02 May central see a shop's sales of goods that central did not supply?

M-03 May central see a shop's locally sourced inventory and the identity of its local suppliers?

M-04 May central see a shop's margins, profitability and shrinkage?

M-05 May one shop see any data belonging to another? If lateral transfers are permitted under C-10, stock
          availability must be visible at minimum. How much beyond that is acceptable?

M-06 Should there be an aggregate-only tier, where central sees anonymised or combined figures for
          planning without seeing shop-identifiable detail?
           Why we ask: This is frequently the compromise that makes federation-wide reporting politically acceptable. It is
           straightforward to build if specified now and awkward to retrofit onto reports already written.

M-07 If a society leaves the federation, what happens to its historical data: deleted, exported to it, or retained
          by central?

M-08 Who administers users at a shop: the shop itself, or central?
           Why we ask: Shop-level administration respects autonomy but distributes the security risk across many small
           organisations with limited IT capability. Central administration is safer but slower and may be resisted.

M-09 Which data protection or privacy obligations apply to member and customer data, and is there a
          designated responsible officer?

M-10 Does any authority, such as the Department of Co-operative Development, have a right of access to the
          data or require returns in a prescribed format?

M-11 Is there an agreed data sharing agreement between central and the societies, or does one need to be
          drafted alongside this project?
           Why we ask: In most federations this document does not yet exist, and the system project is what forces it to be
           written. Identifying that early avoids a legal blocker appearing late.
